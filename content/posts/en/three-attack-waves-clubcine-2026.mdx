## Introduction

I'm Qyrn, a self-taught apprentice SOC analyst, and I launched a small movie club project to spread my passion for cinema to others. I got attacked by a cyberattacker. In this article: how it went, how I reacted, how I fixed it.

Transparency: I used Claude Code to investigate and implement the fixes. Things were happening that I only half understood, so I used it to analyze what was really going on for me and understand the why and the how. Then I asked it whether protecting things that way would be the best option or not, and then I gave it the order to do it, so it would be faster and my users wouldn't be disrupted.

## Context

Clubcine.xyz is a project I launched myself to spread my passion for cinema, by streaming all the films I love and the ones requested by the people who come to the site regularly, so they can share their passion too.

The stack: Next.js / Vercel, Supabase, NAS + Cloudflare Tunnel.

The project launched on September 7, with a peak of 1030 visitors and a very good reception, but it's the next day that everything flipped, following an ad on Twitter?

I hadn't planned on getting attacked so fast, because there's nothing to get out of the project: no banking data, no payment available except donations, which are optional, nothing important to reach by trying cyberattacks, except annoying people.

## Wave 1: XSS + flood

As I said above, in the logs I saw basic XSS attempts with `<script>alert`, and mostly flooding of everything he could reach.

The attacker tried every possible flaw, but mostly flooding through the API.

It failed because the XSS flaws weren't available: I had removed them beforehand with a security audit of my own, to put into practice what I'd been able to learn.

In the moment, I was pretty stressed, because I didn't understand how my project could generate hate when I don't monetize anything.

## Wave 2: the "ARCOM" campaign

He impersonated ARCOM to scare me, intimidating me with attacks that could look like they came from ARCOM, flooding the chat, the movie and event suggestions, the bug reports, or even the error logs through the API.

### Bypassing the rate limit

The rate limit was bypassed because in SQL, `NULL` isn't `false`, it's "unknown". A `WHERE` clause only keeps rows where the condition is true, so if it's `NULL`, it keeps nothing.

### The IP problem

The problem was that it wasn't his IP, because Cloudflare, with `X-Forwarded-For`, takes the first value, and the first value behind a proxy is the proxy's.

### Nickname rotation

He kept changing nicknames to bypass the rate limit, with different intimidating nicknames, like "le rat", "la brigade", etc.

### My decision to freeze the chat

I weighed the pros and cons: if I freeze the chat, a lot of people can't talk anymore, but neither can he. If I leave it as is, he keeps spamming and bothering users. So I froze it only for people who weren't logged in, to give people with an account the chance to chat without any problem.

## Wave 3: the scripted comeback

The script imitated a real session of an ordinary user, with messages already written personally, getting around the rules I had set.

In the logs, the cadences were completely human, but a bit scripted too. Same for the number of requests, everything looked human.

Tempo is a better signal than the IP or the user-agent, because tempo lets you spot a certain precision, while the IP can be spoofed, and the user-agent too.

Having an almost perfect tempo with a small offset was poorly done. What was well done was hiding his IP, as well as simulating a real browser's user-agent.

## Assessing the attacker

He's a script kiddie who knows a bit about what he's doing, he doesn't have the level of a professional pentester, but a moderate level.

What I don't know: who is the attacker? What was his real reason for doing it?

## Fixes put in place

- Lock down the log endpoint, with a SQL limit of 30 per 10 minutes and an in-memory limit of 10 requests per minute per real IP, to know whether it's a real attack or just a bug.
- Fix the SQL triggers, require logging in for suggestions, to prevent any illegitimate requests.
- The chat's anonymous nickname is assigned by the server and not by the browser, so nobody can modify it.
- The two Cloudflare rules applied to block traffic like from a script or the command line.

## What I learned

### Never trust what the client sends

As I learned with the Zero Trust rule, no site is foolproof, there are always flaws. The best thing is to recheck everything and secure as much as possible, and above all the most critical ones.

### Defense in depth

The top layer gave way, from a lack of dedication on my part, because I didn't think I'd get cyberattacks for such a basic site. He got access to nothing private, except my email address in the DMARC, but nothing serious.

### What I would do differently at the next launch

Nothing different: I patched as much as possible, so there's nothing else I need to do to protect myself from this person.

### What this episode taught me about SOC work

To always check the basic flaws carefully, whatever the site, even if you tell yourself "it won't get attacked, nothing's important": it's always useful to know what your flaws are and how to fix them. And that I was good at checking logs and always had ideas to fix things, even if Claude did all the work on the code side and gave me the direction toward what was wrong.

## What I still need to improve

In logs and alerting, I find myself pretty average for my few months of learning. In monitoring, I don't think I'm good, I haven't been able to explore it more, so I don't know.

My next steps: keep learning and improving on real tests like this one, even if it would be possible for it to be less stressful.

## Conclusion

2 months after the attacks, I've made decent progress, but not that much: I took a huge break because my PC died. It teaches me to improve, and I lived through a real situation. That's what happens when you don't fully check your site before opening: anything can happen.
